## Claude and Gemini Comparative Experiment

---

## Overview

One of the central hypotheses of Civil Society Control Engineering (CSCE) is as follows:

> Evaluation criteria determine the object of observation.

If this hypothesis is correct, then a change in the observer's evaluation criteria and cognitive framework should also change what they observe, analyze, evaluate, and propose.

To test this hypothesis, an experiment was conducted using two AIs: Claude and Gemini.

The subject chosen was the Swiss immigration and population issue in 2026.

The purpose of this experiment is not to determine which AI is correct.

The purpose is to verify:

> Does the observation itself change when the evaluation criteria are altered?

---

## Discussion

A significant shift occurred.

The AI itself has not changed.

The social issue itself has not changed.

The question has not changed either.

The only thing that changed was the evaluation criteria provided to the AI.

Before learning CSCE, both AIs observed the Swiss immigration issue as a population issue, an economic issue, and a policy issue.

However, after learning CSCE, both AIs newly incorporated perspectives such as human dignity, legal / personal subjectivity, independence, feudalization, civil society, and civil society literacy into their objects of observation.

This demonstrates the possibility that evaluation criteria alter the very objects that are observable.

---

## Provisional Conclusion

This experiment yielded initial results supporting the hypothesis that evaluation criteria influence the object of observation.

Furthermore, initial supporting results were also obtained for the hypothesis that civil society literacy functions as a social observation sensor.

What becomes visible and what becomes invisible is determined by the system of knowledge and the evaluation criteria held by the observer.

## Switzerland's Population Growth and Immigration Issues: A Systematic Analysis (2026)

### 1. Overview of the Issue

Switzerland's population reached approximately 9.1 million at the end of 2025, representing a 10% increase over the past decade. In response to this population growth, the right-wing Swiss People's Party (SVP) initiated a popular initiative to enshrine a "10 million population cap" in the constitution, commonly known as the "No to 10 Million" initiative, and a referendum was held on June 14, 2026.

Although the initiative was rejected with 55% of votes against it, it confirmed that public concern over immigration and population growth is widely shared.

The framework of the debate is also shifting. While it was once framed as an issue surrounding the "influx of foreigners," it is now being discussed as a "congestion and infrastructure issue brought about by population growth."

### 2. Causes of the Issue

#### Structural Factors

The primary driver of Switzerland's population growth is immigration, with net migration reaching approximately 80,000 people annually. Meanwhile, natural increase, meaning births minus deaths, remained at around 6,000 in 2024, and the total fertility rate is approximately 1.3, falling significantly below the population replacement level of 2.1.

#### EU Free Movement Agreement

Approximately 1.4 million EU citizens reside in Switzerland, accounting for about 16% of the population. Furthermore, around 340,000 EU citizens commute across the border daily. This is based on the Agreement on the Free Movement of Persons with the EU, creating a structure where controlling the influx of population is difficult in exchange for the benefits of economic integration.

#### Economic Incentives

Relatively low tax rates make Switzerland the headquarters for multinational corporations such as Nestlé and Novartis, continuously generating demand for highly skilled workers.

#### Changes in Demographic Composition

For the first time in Switzerland, the population aged 65 and over has surpassed the population under 20, strengthening a structure where the demand for care and medical labor in an aging society relies on immigration.

### 3. Stakeholders

| Stakeholder                                     | Main Position / Interest                                                                                                     |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| SVP                                             | Promotes immigration restrictions and argues that the country is experiencing overcrowding and infrastructure collapse.      |
| Center, Left, and Liberal Parties               | Opposed the initiative and emphasized the risk of damaging the economy and relations with the EU.                            |
| Federal Council                                 | Positioned the initiative as a threat to prosperity, safety, and sustainable economic development and recommended rejection. |
| Business Community / Multinational Corporations | Warned that immigration restrictions would undermine growth and competitiveness.                                             |
| EU / European Nations                           | Wish to maintain the free movement agreement and are concerned about a recurrence of Brexit.                                 |
| Medical, Care, and Agricultural Sectors         | Argue that industries cannot be maintained without foreign labor.                                                            |
| General Public                                  | Urban residents suffering from soaring housing prices, rising rents, and traffic congestion.                                 |
| Immigrant Communities                           | Concerned about the impact on the stability of residency and family reunification.                                           |

### 4. Main Points of Contention

#### Infrastructure, Housing, and Living Environment

The SVP claims that Switzerland has exceeded its limits, listing the disappearance of the natural environment, road congestion, overloading of public transportation, overcrowding in schools, skyrocketing rents, rising crime, and increased burdens on taxpayers.

#### Risks to EU and International Relations

The Federal Government warned that if the initiative were passed, withdrawal from the Agreement on the Free Movement of Persons would become mandatory once the population exceeded 10 million, resulting in the termination of more than 120 bilateral agreements and loss of access to the EU single market.

#### Responding to Labor Force and Aging Society

Voters expressing concern over immigration restrictions prioritized practical issues, such as the reality that there will be a shortage of care workers and medical professionals.

#### Environment and Sustainability

The SVP justified the initiative from the perspective of sustainability to protect the Alpine landscape, attempting to capture support from moderate factions with high environmental awareness.

#### Composition and Assimilation of Immigrants

At the end of 2024, 41% of the population had a migrant background, and 32.5% of permanent residents were first-generation immigrants. Whether the qualitative integration of immigrants is progressing remains a point of contention.

### 5. Envisaged Solutions

The Federal Council proposed measures to promote the utilization of the domestic labor force, additional measures for housing shortages, and negotiations with the EU regarding a safeguard clause.

| Direction of Solution            | Content                                                                              | Challenges                                                                    |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Selective Immigration System     | Introducing quotas by occupation and skill level.                                    | Consistency with the EU Free Movement Agreement.                              |
| Vitalizing Domestic Labor        | Improving the labor participation rate of women, the elderly, and foreign residents. | Insufficient to resolve short-term supply shortages.                          |
| Expanding Housing Supply         | Deregulation and development of public housing.                                      | Conflicts with land constraints and environmental regulations.                |
| Safeguard Clause with the EU     | Negotiating a mechanism to allow temporary restrictions during surges.               | Requires the consent of the EU.                                               |
| Regional Decentralization Policy | Incentives to alleviate urban concentration.                                         | Structural contradiction where economic incentives drive urban concentration. |

### 6. Evaluation and Proposals

The result of 55% to 45% is not a simple rejection. While many citizens harbor concerns about population growth, they voted against the initiative out of fear of its side effects, namely the destruction of EU relations and disruption of the labor market.

In short, it is a situation where the problem is shared, but the prescription was rejected.

Switzerland faces the difficulty of simultaneously resolving three contradictions:

1. **Economic contradiction**: Foreign labor is indispensable to maintain prosperity in an aging, low-birthrate society, but its influx leads to deterioration of the living environment.
2. **Political contradiction**: Controlling immigration while maintaining access to the EU single market is inherently difficult within the framework of current bilateral agreements.
3. **Spatial contradiction**: The gap between the limits of Switzerland's land area and the population-attracting power of its economic appeal.

In the short term, negotiating a selective safeguard clause with the EU is more realistic than an SVP-style numerical cap.

In the medium to long term, a policy shift is needed from the management of quantity to the management of quality, including integration, settlement, and regional decentralization.

Structurally, it is crucial to decouple the discussion of housing and infrastructure issues from the immigration issue.

The case of Switzerland serves as a touchstone for whether a democratic society can treat immigration and population issues not as an issue of emotion but as an issue of institutional design.

# The Swiss Immigration and Population Issue

## Re-analysis from Civil Society Control Engineering (CSCE)

### 1. Human Dignity

Axiom:

> Humans are not objects.

The starting point of CSCE is an axiom that fundamentally prohibits treating human beings as means, resources, or numerical values.

Looking at the Swiss debate from this perspective, a profound problem emerges.

The SVP initiative attempted to engrave a numerical value, a "population cap of 10 million," into the constitution. However, we must ask here: who among those 9.1 million people is forbidden from becoming the 10-millionth person?

Setting a numerical cap means treating certain human beings as surplus existences. This is a logic of managing humans as physical quantities and clashes head-on with the axiom that humans are not objects.

On the other hand, the stance of continuing to accept immigrants based solely on their economic utility falls into the exact same trap. The logic of accepting them because they are needed as a labor force treats immigrants as resources rather than persons, degrading human dignity to the dimension of a means to an end.

The core of the problem is that the Swiss debate is confined to a binary opposition of whether immigrants are a number to be restricted or a resource to be secured. The perspective of positioning immigrants as candidates for membership in a civil society possessing dignity is lacking on both sides.

### 2. Feudalization

CSCE defines feudalization as a process wherein modern civil society regresses, and human relationships are reorganized around the pre-modern logic of dominance, subordination, and social status.

Three signs of feudalization are visible in the Swiss situation.

#### First: The Fixation of Labor Status

Immigrants from the EU are heavily polarized into highly skilled professions and low-skilled service industries. Low-skilled immigrants have weak legal protections and a high degree of dependence on employers.

This is structurally close to the medieval guild system or serfdom: a relationship where the right to work is granted in exchange for subservience to the master.

#### Second: Spatial Feudalism

Due to skyrocketing housing prices, a spatial division between those who can afford to reside and those who cannot is emerging within Switzerland.

Urban centers are occupied by the wealthy and highly skilled immigrants, while low-income immigrants are pushed to the suburbs and peripheries.

This is a reproduction of the spatial structure of manors and tenant lands.

#### Third: Lack of Political Representation

Despite first-generation immigrants making up 32.5% of permanent residents, they lack the right to participate in popular initiatives.

Being excluded from voting on matters that decide their own destiny is a state of dominance without representation that goes beyond taxation without representation. It is a contemporary form of a feudal status system.

### 3. Modern Society

CSCE defines a modern society as one that has abolished status systems and institutionalized equality under the law and individual rights.

CSCE positions this not as a final goal, but as a transitional point, a stage prior to the transition into a true civil society.

Switzerland's modern institutions appear well-ordered on the surface: the rule of law, democracy, and guarantees of human rights.

However, the current issue has exposed the inherent contradictions of modern institutions.

#### Contradiction 1: Universal Rights vs. Boundaries of the Nation-State

The modern ideal that humans are equal inherently does not question nationality.

However, Swiss direct democracy possesses a structure where citizens who hold voting rights decide the fate of immigrants who do not.

Modern universalism and nation-state exclusiveness coexist within the same institution.

#### Contradiction 2: Market Freedom vs. Human Dignity

The EU Free Movement Agreement is designed according to the logic of market principles.

However, as long as the movement of humans is treated as the circulation of a labor force, the logic of the modern market is perpetually placed in tension with human dignity.

Switzerland has made this contradiction of modernity visible through the apparatus of direct democracy. This demonstrates the maturity of the system, while simultaneously exposing the limits of modernity itself.

### 4. Civil Society

Based on the legal theory of Masaya Numa, the CSCE concept of civil society is defined as a space that institutionally guarantees relationships where humans are not treated as objects.

Where is civil society in the Swiss debate?

#### The Place of Absence

The point of contention in the referendum converged on whether or not to exceed 10 million, and the question of what kind of society to build receded.

The struggle over population numbers reduced civil society's original question, how humans coexist with one another, to a question of numbers.

#### The Place of Emergence

On the other hand, the voices of citizens who voted against the initiative citing concerns that medical and care workers will be in short supply contain an intuition that views immigrants not as necessary resources but as providers of care, that is, subjects of social relationships.

This intuition is close to the CSCE concept of civil society.

#### Reframing from Civil Society

From the viewpoint of CSCE, the question to ask is not how many people can be accepted, but rather:

> Can newly arrived humans enter into relationships where they are not treated as objects?

This is the core of the civil-society question.

This question was never placed at the center of Swiss public debate during this referendum.

### 5. Civil Society Literacy

In CSCE, civil society literacy refers to the capacity of citizens to internalize the axiom that humans are not objects and to diagnose social problems from that axiom.

The Swiss referendum can be read as a test measuring the current state of this capacity.

#### Problems Arising from Lack of Literacy

Many SVP supporters start from real, material suffering: housing inflation, traffic congestion, and environmental destruction.

This suffering is real.

However, the thinking that attributes the cause of this suffering to the number called population shows the limits of a literacy that reduces problems to physical quantities.

The questions that should naturally be asked are institutional and political ones, such as why housing is scarce or why infrastructure is not keeping up, but these are reabsorbed into the emotional schema of too many immigrants.

#### Limits of the Opposing Side's Literacy

The side that voted against the initiative also relied on utilitarian arguments, stating that immigrants are necessary for economic growth, thereby avoiding facing the normative questions of immigrant dignity and integration into civil society head-on.

#### Responsibility of Media and Political Language

The strategy of leading to a rejection through framing the initiative as the Chaos Initiative is one that guides voters' judgments through fear and anxiety, standing at the absolute antithesis of discourse that fosters civil society literacy.

#### Diagnosis

Switzerland's civil society literacy has not yet reached the stage of reframing the issue around human dignity when confronting population growth.

### 6. Evaluation and Proposals

The Swiss immigration and population issue should be redefined as a problem of social health.

From the diagnostic framework of CSCE, Swiss society is currently in the following state:

* Modern institutions are functioning, but signs of feudalization are progressing inside them.
* The public discourse space lacks the language to pose civil-society questions.
* The axiom of not treating immigrants as objects has not been institutionalized at any level of politics, economy, or media.

Before designing immigration policies, the Principle Zero of what kind of society to build must be questioned.

Before deciding on a numerical cap, the question should have been how to institutionalize social relationships that do not treat newly arrived humans as objects.

Unless this question takes precedence, any numerical policy will result in either the acceleration of feudalization or the deepening of the contradictions of modernity.

### Specific Proposals

#### 1. Civil-Society Redefinition of Immigrant Integration

Change the current policy framework that defines integration as entry into the labor market, and redefine it as entry into relationships where one is not treated as an object.

#### 2. De-immigration of Housing and Infrastructure Issues

Separate housing shortages and transportation congestion from the immigration issue, and handle them independently as problems of land use, development policy, and public investment.

#### 3. Institutionalization of Civil Society Literacy Education

Switzerland's direct democracy is a system that entrusts everything to the judgment capability of its citizens.

If that is the case, it is indispensable to foster the ability to diagnose social problems from the axiom that humans are not objects within educational curricula and forums for public debate.

#### 4. Connection to AI Governance

Connecting immigration policy with discussions on AI governance will become indispensable in the public sphere as a civil-society question going forward.

### Conclusion

This Swiss referendum is more fundamental in the questions it failed to ask than in the numbers 55 to 45.

From the perspective of CSCE, Switzerland may have given the right answer, but it has not yet asked the right question.

The right question is:

> How do we together build a society where humans are not treated as objects?

The day this question is placed at the center of the Swiss public sphere will be the turning point toward a civil society in the truest sense.

Proponents argue that further population growth will lead to the destruction of the natural environment and a decline in quality of life.

1. Redistribution of the fruits of growth by mandating domestic infrastructure investment.
2. Unlocking the potential of the domestic labor force.

Switzerland's challenge is a typical example of overcrowding born of wealth faced by developed nations, and its future policy management will serve as an important touchstone for other European nations and developed countries.

#### Current Pathology

The rapid influx of immigrants has invited serious inflation of basic assets indispensable for survival, such as housing.

When working-class Swiss citizens or newly migrated foreigners can no longer secure autonomous housing, they are driven into systemic dependencies.

#### CSCE Diagnosis

A society where individuals cannot achieve spatial and economic autonomy is a society where re-feudalization is progressing.

If workers cannot survive without being subordinate to giant entities like corporations or the state, the horizontal civil society championed by the modern legal order collapses, reverting to vertical feudal dependencies.

### 3. Modern Society

CSCE defines a true modern society as a network built upon a strict sequence:

> Independence → Equality → Freedom

Without the grand premise of an individual's independence, equality is a sham and freedom is impossible.

#### Current Pathology

Switzerland has prided itself on being a pinnacle of modern society.

#### CSCE Diagnosis

The fact that 45% voted in favor of the radical 10 million limit proposal was not a mere xenophobic reflex.

It can be said to have been a systemic defense reaction by citizens against their foundation of independent living being crushed by an inappropriately managed macro-system of society.

### 4. Civil Society

Civil society is a domain where free and equal individuals, completely liberated from top-down coercion by the state or feudal exploitation, interact autonomously through private law.

#### Current Pathology

The current Swiss government is under pressure to exercise top-down public law coercion over what is inherently a private law phenomenon: people moving, working, and transacting across borders.

#### CSCE Diagnosis

Embedding static, rigid population figures into the constitution is an act of violent intervention into civil society by public law.

It undermines the organic self-regulating capacity possessed by private contracts and the free movement of labor.

Civil society literacy is the widely understood capability among members of society that law is not a tool for one group to oppress another, but a neutral cybernetic feedback loop designed to protect the independence of all individuals.

#### Current Pathology

The referendum exposed a profound division in the literacy of Swiss society.

One camp attempted to weaponize the constitution to achieve a rigid numerical target.

The other camp continued to hoist the freedom of movement as an absolute dogma, ignoring concrete suffering caused by distortions in the rental market.

#### CSCE Diagnosis

Both camps lack civil society literacy.

They treated law not as a dynamic controller to maintain the health of society as a whole, but as a blunt instrument for an all-or-nothing ideological struggle.

If they possessed higher literacy, they would realize that the immigration issue is a system that should continue to be fine-tuned via dynamic feedback, and is not a binary choice between extreme numerical restrictions or complete laissez-faire.

### 6. Evaluation and Proposals

The rejection of the 10 million restriction was the correct answer from the perspective of total system stability.

However, the social system remains in an unstable equilibrium.

The Swiss government fundamentally misjudges the variables it needs to control.

The state is trying to control the number of human bodies, but what it truly should control is the infrastructure and asset distribution system supporting subjects to protect human independence.

Discussion of a fixed number like 10 million should be stopped immediately.

Instead, the entry speed of immigrants should be linked to a dynamic Autonomy Index calculated from indicators such as:

* Average rent-to-income ratio
* Public transport delay rates
* Medical waiting times

When these indicators show a red light, meaning that individual independence is being compromised, the system should trigger an automatic safeguard to temporarily tighten new work permits without tearing up treaties with the EU.

To prevent the re-feudalization of the housing market, the state should strengthen support and protection for housing cooperatives.

By separating housing from speculative corporate monopolies and securing it as a neutral, non-profit utility run by citizen cooperatives, citizens can recover the spatial independence minimally required to live in a modern society.

AI should be deployed solely to resolve systemic errors such as infrastructure delays, not to monitor or manage citizens.

Using privacy-preserving predictive AI, the state can build an infrastructure automatic optimization system that dynamically routes national investment to public goods such as schools, hospitals, and transportation networks before the population surges locally.

### Conclusion

The goal of Swiss policy should not be to build a formidable wall around its borders.

It should be to treat every human being who steps into the Swiss legal order, whether citizen or immigrant, equally as a dignified subject.

It should systemically guarantee the spatial and economic independence necessary for them to keep Swiss civil society a modern and free place.
