## 1. Why Legal Science Requires a Working Definition of Law

Numa Theory has previously established that law is a distinct type of social norm—intertwined with the ideal of justice and structurally separated from customs, morality, and religion. 

However, when engaging in legal science as a rigorous academic discipline, we cannot rely on vague intuitions. We must establish a clear boundary line to determine what falls within the scope of legal analysis and what remains outside it. 

Numa Theory approaches this challenge not by seeking a rigid, metaphysical "absolute essence" of law, but by constructing a **working concept (作業概念)**. A working definition serves as a precise methodological tool, allowing scholars to clearly identify, classify, and analyze legal phenomena within historical and contemporary social realities without getting lost in abstract ontological debates.

---

## 2. The Logical Formula: Genus and Specific Difference

To construct a scientifically rigorous definition, Numa Theory utilizes the classical logical formula inherited from traditional Aristotelian logic:

$$\text{Definition} = \text{Genus (上位概念)} + \text{Specific Difference (種差)}$$

This method isolates an object by first placing it within a broader category and then defining the unique characteristics that distinguish it from everything else in that same category.

### The Pitfall of Omitting Specific Difference
If a definition only states the broad category, it fails to define the object. For instance, defining a cow merely as a "four-legged animal" is useless because it fails to exclude horses, deer, or dogs. 

To define "law" accurately, legal science must systematically isolate its broad category (Genus) and its unique distinguishing features (Specific Difference).

## 3. Critiquing Traditional Definitions of Law

Before presenting its own framework, Numa Theory evaluates and critiques standard definitions prevalent in traditional legal jurisprudence:

### Critique 1: "A norm commanded and enforced by the state."
* **The Flaw:** This definition reduces law entirely to a mere tool of state command. It ignores the deep social character of law, its historical roots in customary interactions, and its internal connection to the ideal of justice.

### Critique 2: "An enforceable norm that regulates external human behavior."
* **The Flaw:** While this captures the "external grasp" of law, it remains a purely formalistic and mechanical definition. It treats law as a technical apparatus of compulsion, failing to account for the substantive purpose of modern law—the realization of civil society.

## 4. The Numa Theory Definition: Mapping Genus and Specific Difference

By synthesizing the structural insights of Civil Society Control Engineering, Numa Theory defines law through a precise dual-layered structure:

### 1. The Genus (上位概念): A Social Norm belonging to *Sollen*
Law belongs to the broader category of **social norms (社会規範)**. It is part of the world of *Sollen* ("what ought to be"), sharing this Genus with customs, morality, and religion. It is a prescriptive framework designed to guide human behavior.

### 2. The Specific Difference (種差): State-Backed Compulsion for Civil Coexistence
What sets law apart from all other social norms consists of three distinct characteristics:
- **Focus on External Interaction:** It regulates the external relations and boundaries of action between individuals, rather than searching their inward spiritual conscience.
- **State-Organized Compulsion:** It is backed by the organized, institutionalized compulsive power of the state to enforce compliance or sanction violations.
- **The Modern Civil Purpose:** It treats all concrete, biological human beings as abstract and equal legal persons before the law to guarantee a social order where individuals can coexist as independent, equal, and free subjects.

|---|---|

## 5. The Core Definition of Law

By unifying Genus and Specific Difference, Numa Theory delivers its definitive, streamlined definition:

> **Law is a state-enforced social norm that regulates external human behavior to establish and protect a civil society where every individual can live as an independent, equal, and free subject.**

Law is never a self-justifying system of pure power, nor is it a mere academic exercise in textual interpretation. It is the indispensable, compulsive machinery utilized by civil society to transform the ideal of human dignity into an enforceable reality.
