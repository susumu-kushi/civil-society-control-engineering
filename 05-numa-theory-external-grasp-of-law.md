# Numa Theory: The External Grasp of Law
## How Law Is Distinguished from Other Social Norms

---

## Core Principle

> Human beings must never be treated merely as objects.

---

## 1. What Is the "External Grasp" of Law?

Before entering the substance of law itself, Numa Theory begins by stepping back and looking at law **from the outside**.

Society contains many kinds of norms: morality, religion, custom, etiquette, and more.
Among all of these, why is "law" alone singled out as the object of a distinct academic discipline — legal science?

To answer this question, it is necessary to examine what we mean by "law" before we study it.
This is what Numa Theory calls the **external grasp of law**: approaching law from the outside, clarifying what it is and how it differs from other norms, before examining its inner structure.

This step is not merely preliminary.
It is the foundation of rigorous legal thought.

> Numa Theory's principle: legal science begins with classification.
> Before studying law, we must know what law is — and what it is not.

---

## 2. Law and Legal Science

Legal science is the study of law.
But the word "law" is not simple.

In everyday language, "law" can refer to:
- Religious teachings (the law of the Buddha, divine law)
- Moral principles
- Laws of nature (the law of gravity)
- Legal rules enacted by the state

These are very different things.
Legal science cannot study all of them at once.

Numa Theory therefore begins by **distinguishing the object of legal science** — law in the legal sense — from the many other things that the word "law" might denote.

The key distinction is between two levels of meaning:

> **Law in the formal sense** and **Law in the substantive sense**

---

## 3. Law in the Formal Sense

Law in the **formal sense** means: **a norm enacted through a prescribed procedure**.

Examples:
- A statute passed by a legislature and promulgated
- A regulation issued by an administrative authority

The defining characteristic of law in the formal sense is **how it was made** — not what it says.

From this perspective, a tyrannical decree and a democratic constitution are both equally "law."
What matters is that they were produced through the recognized form of lawmaking.

This is a deliberately neutral, procedural concept.
It sets aside the question of whether the law is just or unjust, good or bad.

The purpose of this concept is precision: to give legal science a clear and determinate object — **the body of norms produced through recognized legal processes**.

---

## 4. Law in the Substantive Sense

Law in the **substantive sense** asks a different question:

**What kind of norms can truly be called "law"?**

This is not a procedural question. It is a question of value and content.

- Can a regulation that ignores human rights — that treats persons as objects — genuinely be called "law"?
- What content must a norm have to deserve recognition as law in a modern civil society?

Pursuing this question leads directly to the great declarations of the modern era:

The French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen.
The American Declaration of Independence.

Their proclamation:

> **All human beings are born free and equal.**

This is the modern conception of the human person and of civil society — and it is, in Numa Theory's view, the substantive core of modern law.

Law in the substantive sense is law that **embodies and protects** this principle.
A norm that denies independence, equality, and freedom — that reduces human beings to objects — may be "law" in the formal sense, but fails the test of substantive law.

This distinction between formal and substantive law is the foundation upon which Numa Theory's central sequence rests:

> **Independence → Equality → Freedom**

---

## 5. Law and Legal Proposition — A Further Distinction

Numa Theory draws one further distinction within the concept of law:

### Law (Norm)
The norm itself — the rule that directs human behavior.
"Do this." "Do not do that." "This right belongs to this person."

### Legal Proposition (Statement)
The linguistic expression of the norm — the text of a statute, a court's statement of a rule, a scholar's explanation of what the law requires.

These two are theoretically distinct.

The text of a statute and the norm it expresses are not identical.
The same norm can be expressed in different words.
The same words can be interpreted to express different norms.

This distinction matters enormously for legal interpretation — for understanding how courts read statutes, how scholars analyze precedents, and how law develops over time.

---

## 6. The Principle of Classification in Legal Science

Numa Theory treats the external grasp of law as an instance of a general principle:

> **Legal science is, fundamentally, the science of classification.**

To study anything rigorously, one must first:
1. Define a **unifying concept** — the object of study
2. Establish **criteria of distinction** — what falls within the concept and what does not
3. Apply those criteria consistently

In the case of law:
- The unifying concept is "law" — the body of norms that constitute legal order
- The criteria of distinction separate law from morality, religion, custom, and other social norms
- Within law, further distinctions are drawn: formal / substantive, norm / proposition, public / private, and so on

This method of classification — defining, distinguishing, and organizing — runs through the entire structure of Numa Theory.

---

## 7. Why These Distinctions Matter for Civil Society

The distinction between formal and substantive law is not merely academic.
It has direct consequences for the defense of civil society.

**The danger of formal law without substance:**
If law is defined purely by procedure — if anything enacted through proper form counts as law — then there is no basis for criticizing unjust laws.
The laws of apartheid, the Nuremberg laws, the legal frameworks of feudal domination — all were "formally valid."
A purely formal concept of law cannot condemn them.

**The necessity of substantive law:**
Numa Theory insists that law must be evaluated against a standard: the ideals of civil society — independence, equality, and freedom.
A norm that violates these ideals may be formally valid, but it is substantively deficient — and it is the task of legal science to say so.

This is why Numa Theory characterizes legal science not as mere rule-interpretation, but as:

> **The ongoing critical analysis of legal order in light of the ideals of civil society.**

## 8. The Two Forms of Legal Compulsion

From the external grasp of law, Numa Theory identifies two forms through which law shapes human behavior:

### Compulsion That Gives (与える強制)
Law that provides, supplements, and supports — ensuring that every person has the resources to exercise their independence.
Examples: maintenance obligations, social welfare, legal representatives for those who cannot act alone.

### Compulsion That Takes (奪う強制)
Law that restricts, prohibits, and penalizes — setting limits on the freedom of the powerful to protect the independence of the vulnerable.
Examples: criminal law, anti-monopoly law, labor protection, consumer protection.

Together, these two forms of compulsion express the dual structure of modern law:
- **Technology of competition** — enabling free and equal persons to pursue their own interests
- **Ethics of mutual assistance** — protecting those who cannot stand alone

The external grasp of law — the clear identification of what law is and what it does — makes it possible to evaluate whether any particular legal system is actually serving these purposes.
