# Numa Theory: Law and the Laws of Nature
## The Distinction Between Norm and Fact — Sein and Sollen

---

## Core Principle

> Human beings must never be treated merely as objects.

---

## 1. Law and Natural Laws Are Fundamentally Different

The word "law" is used in many senses.
The law of gravity. The laws of supply and demand. The laws of a state.

Numa Theory insists on a fundamental distinction:
**Natural laws and legal laws belong to entirely different logical categories.**

### Natural Laws (Laws of Nature)
Natural laws — the law of gravity, the laws of physics and chemistry — are **descriptive propositions**.
They describe how the world *is*.

"Under certain conditions, this will always happen."

No one can "violate" a natural law.
It simply describes what occurs, necessarily and without exception.

### Legal Laws (Legal Norms)
Legal laws are **normative propositions**.
They prescribe how human beings *ought* to behave.

"You must do this."
"You must not do that."
"This right belongs to this person."

A legal norm can be obeyed — or violated.
When it is violated, a response follows: sanction, liability, enforcement.

> The possibility of violation is not a defect of law.
> It is part of law's nature.
> Law addresses free human beings — beings who can choose to comply or not.

| | Natural Law | Legal Law |
|---|---|---|
| Type | Descriptive (Sein — what is) | Normative (Sollen — what ought to be) |
| Violation | Impossible | Possible — and legally consequential |
| Object | Physical events | Human behavior |
| Purpose | Explanation | Direction |

## 2. Social Laws Are Also Distinct from Legal Norms

Economics, sociology, and the social sciences identify regularities in social life:
"When demand increases, prices tend to rise."
"Concentrated power tends toward abuse."

These are **social laws** — patterns observed in the behavior of societies and markets.

Like natural laws, social laws are descriptive.
They explain what tends to happen — not what ought to happen.
They carry no command. They issue no obligation.

Legal norms are different.

A legal norm does not merely describe what people do.
It directs what people **must** do — and establishes consequences for those who do not.

No matter how precise a social science becomes, it does not thereby become legal science.
Legal science is concerned with **norms that guide human action** — not with the empirical regularities of social behavior.

---

## 3. Law as Normative Proposition

Numa Theory frames law as a specific kind of proposition:

> "Under these conditions, a person ought to behave in this way."

This proposition always contains an **ought** (Sollen).
It is not a description of what happens.
It is a direction of what should happen.

This logical structure — the "ought" embedded in every legal norm — is what separates legal science from natural science and social science.

Natural science asks: **What is the case?**
Legal science asks: **What ought to be done?**

This is why legal science cannot be separated from questions of value and justice.
To ask what people ought to do is always, implicitly, to ask what is right — and what is wrong.

## 4. Sein and Sollen — The Living Society and Civil Society

This distinction between descriptive fact (Sein — what is) and normative ideal (Sollen — what ought to be) is one of the most important conceptual tools in Numa Theory.

### The Living Society (Sein)
If we observe human beings biologically and socially — as they actually are — we find a world shaped by two competing forces:

**Competition for survival:**
The drive to acquire resources, to exclude rivals, to advance one's own interests at the expense of others.

**Mutual assistance:**
The drive to care for children, to cooperate, to share labor, to support those who cannot support themselves.

These two forces intersect and conflict in every human society.
This is the world as it is — the living society of Sein.

This world exists prior to law.
It is the raw material that law must work with.

### The Civil Society (Sollen)
Civil society — the society in which every person is treated as independent, equal, and free — is not a description of the world as it is.
It is an ideal: a vision of how human society **ought** to be organized.

This ideal is expressed in the great principles of modern law:
- Every person possesses legal capacity from birth
- No person may be reduced to an object of power, profit, or control
- Independence, equality, and freedom belong to all

**Modern law is the bridge between Sein and Sollen.**

It takes the living society — full of competition, inequality, and vulnerability — as its starting point.
And it works, through legal norms and institutions, to bring that reality closer to the ideal of civil society.

## 5. The Two Forms of Legal Compulsion — Revisited

The bridge between Sein and Sollen is built through two forms of legal compulsion:

### Compulsion That Gives (与える強制)
Addressing the reality that some persons cannot stand alone — due to age, disability, poverty, or social exclusion — law **provides** what they need to exercise their independence.

Legal representatives for those who cannot act alone.
Maintenance obligations and social welfare for those without resources.
Education, healthcare, and social security as foundations for genuine freedom.

This form of compulsion responds to the force of **mutual assistance** — institutionalizing the social instinct to support the vulnerable.

### Compulsion That Takes (奪う強制)
Addressing the reality that the powerful will exploit the weak if left unconstrained, law **restricts** what the powerful may do.

Criminal law. Anti-monopoly regulation. Labor protection. Consumer protection.
Limits on property rights when they destroy the livelihoods of others.

This form of compulsion responds to the force of **competition** — setting the boundaries within which competition must operate to remain compatible with the independence and equality of all.

Together, these two forms of compulsion transform the living society of Sein into something closer to the civil society of Sollen.

## 6. Why Legal Science Cannot Be Value-Neutral

The distinction between Sein and Sollen has a further implication:

**Legal science cannot be purely descriptive.**

A legal scholar who only describes the norms that exist — without evaluating whether they serve the ideals of civil society — is not doing legal science in the full sense.

Numa Theory characterizes legal science as:

> **The critical analysis of legal order in light of the ideals of civil society — independence, equality, and freedom.**

This means:
- Analyzing reality as it is (Sein) — the actual distribution of power, wealth, and legal protection
- Evaluating that reality against the ideal (Sollen) — does this legal order genuinely protect the independence, equality, and freedom of every person?
- Reconstructing law where it falls short — proposing reforms, identifying contradictions, defending the principles of civil society

A legal science that abandons this critical function becomes a tool of whoever holds power.
It describes the law as it is — and thereby legitimizes it, however unjust.

Numa Theory refuses this abdication.

## 7. The Significance of This Distinction for the AI Era

The Sein / Sollen distinction has direct relevance for the governance of artificial intelligence.

**AI systems operate in the domain of Sein.**
They observe, analyze, and predict human behavior.
They identify patterns in social data.
They optimize outcomes according to defined metrics.

But optimization is not the same as justice.
Efficiency is not the same as freedom.
Prediction is not the same as human dignity.

AI systems that reduce human beings to predictable data points — that treat persons as objects to be optimized and managed — are operating in the domain of Sein while ignoring the demands of Sollen.

Numa Theory's response is clear:

> The task of legal science — and of every institution that governs human life — is not to describe what is, but to realize what ought to be.
> The standard of "what ought to be" is civil society: the independence, equality, and freedom of every person.
> No technology, however powerful, may substitute its own logic for that standard.
