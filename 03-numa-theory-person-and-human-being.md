# Numa Theory: Person and Human Being
## The Legal Construction of the Individual in Civil Society

---

## Core Principle

> Human beings must never be treated merely as objects.

---

## 1. "Human Being" and "Person" Are Not the Same

Ordinary language uses "human being" and "person" as if they were interchangeable.
Numa Theory insists they are not.

This distinction is not merely academic.
It is the foundation upon which modern law stands.

### Human Being
A biological and social existence — shaped by sex, age, physical strength, intelligence, wealth, race, and historical circumstance.
Concrete. Full of natural and social differences.
Far from the ideal of independence, equality, and freedom.

### Person (Legal Person)
Not the biological individual, but a **normative construction of law**.
The natural and social attributes of the human being are temporarily set aside.
What remains is an abstract, standard subject: **an independent, equal, and free bearer of rights and duties**.

When legal scholars say "person," they do not mean the living human being.
They mean this idealized legal figure — constructed by law, for the purposes of law.

> Numa Theory's starting point: law does not take human beings as they are.
> It constructs a standard model of the person — and then works to bring every human being up to that standard.

---

## 2. Why Law Creates an Abstract "Person"

In feudal society, persons were classified by status, blood, and rank.
A person's legal standing depended on who they were born to and where they were born.

Modern law broke with this structure.

To treat every individual as **equally capable of holding rights and bearing duties**, modern law needed a concept that could apply universally — regardless of natural or social difference.

That concept is the legal "person": a standard model of the human individual, abstracted from concrete particularity.

This abstraction is not a denial of human difference.
It is a legal guarantee that **difference does not determine dignity**.

---

## 3. The Legal Person as Rights-Bearing Subject

The legal "person" is not merely an abstraction — it is a subject equipped with specific legal capacities:

**Legal capacity (権利能力)**
The capacity to hold rights and bear duties.
Under modern civil law, every person possesses this capacity from birth.
This is the concrete legal expression of the principle of equality.

**Capacity to act (行為能力)**
The capacity to perform legal acts — to enter into contracts, make declarations of will, and take responsibility for one's own legal transactions.

The legal person is thus both:
- A **subject to whom rights belong** (rights-holder)
- A **subject who acts** in law (agent)

And crucially, this person is conceived first and foremost as an **independent individual** — before being a member of any community or group.

This independence is the premise upon which Numa Theory's sequence is built:

> **Independence → Equality → Freedom**

## 4. Natural Persons and Legal Persons

Modern law recognizes two kinds of legal "person":

### Natural Person
The individual human being.
Every living person possesses legal capacity as a matter of right — not as a privilege granted by the state, but as an expression of the principle that every human being is to be treated as a person.

Under modern law, even the unborn child holds certain rights in limited circumstances.
The principle is clear: **to be human is to be a person in law**.

### Legal Person (Juridical Person)
In modern society, not only individuals but **collective entities** must be able to act in law.
States, municipalities, corporations, cooperatives, foundations — all require the capacity to hold rights, enter contracts, and bear obligations.

Modern law therefore creates a **second kind of "person"**: the legal person (法人).
A legal person is a collective entity to which law grants the status of a rights-bearing subject.
It has a name, a purpose, organs of governance, and property — and is treated as a single "person" in law.

The legal world thus contains two inhabitants:

| Type | Basis |
|---|---|
| Natural person | The individual human being |
| Legal person | A collective entity granted legal personality by law |

Both are equally "persons" in the sense that matters: **bearers of rights and duties**.
Both fall under the ideals of modern law — independence, equality, and freedom.

## 5. The Gap Between "Human Being" and "Person" — and Law's Task

The legal "person" is an ideal.
The living "human being" is a reality full of inequality and vulnerability.

Between these two lies a gap — and it is law's task to close it.

Modern law does this through two mechanisms:

### Supplementing Those Who Cannot Act Alone
Some human beings cannot exercise legal capacity on their own:
- Infants, young children
- Persons with severe cognitive or mental disabilities

For these persons, law assigns a **legal representative** — so that they are not left unprotected in the competition of life.

### Supporting Those Without External Resources
Legal capacity alone is not enough.
A person without property, income, or social support cannot exercise their rights in any meaningful sense.

Law therefore provides — through maintenance obligations, social welfare, and redistributive mechanisms — the **external resources** without which independence is merely formal.

These two mechanisms reflect the two instincts that Numa Theory places at the heart of modern law:

- **Competition** — the drive to pursue one's own interests as a free and equal person
- **Mutual assistance** — the drive to support those who cannot stand alone

## 6. The Political Dimension of the Legal "Person"

Numa Theory emphasizes that the legal concept of "person" is not a neutral technical device.
It is a **political and historical achievement**.

The great declarations of the modern era — the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, the American Declaration of Independence — proclaimed that all human beings are born free and equal.

These declarations elevated the legal concept of "person" into a **political slogan**.

But there has always been a gap between the proclamation and the reality:
- Racial discrimination
- Gender discrimination
- Class exclusion
- Colonial subjugation

All of these represent the persistence of feudal thinking within nominally modern societies — the treatment of certain human beings as less than full persons.

Numa Theory's response is not to abandon the ideal of the legal person.
It is to insist that the gap between "human being" and "person" must be **continuously identified, challenged, and closed** — through law, through institutions, and through the ongoing work of civil society.

---

## 7. The Legal Person in the Age of AI

In the age of artificial intelligence, the gap between "human being" and "person" takes new forms:

- Algorithmic systems that classify and rank human beings as data points
- Automated decisions that determine access to credit, employment, housing, and justice
- Surveillance infrastructures that reduce persons to profiles of predictable behavior

These systems do not recognize the legal "person."
They process the "human being" — in all their concrete particularity — as an object to be optimized, managed, and controlled.

Numa Theory identifies this as a **new form of feudalism**: the reduction of persons to status, determined not by birth but by data.

The legal and ethical response is the same as it has always been:

> Every human being must be recognized as an independent, equal, and free person.
> No system — however powerful, however efficient — may reduce a human being to merely an object.
